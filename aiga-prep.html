📋 Exam Format & Time Allocation

Exam Format

  • 100 total questions: 85 scored + 15 unscored (pretest) questions — unscored items are mixed in, indistinguishable from scored ones, and don't count toward your result, so answer every question with full effort
  • All questions are multiple choice with one correct answer among four options; unanswered questions count as incorrect and there's no extra penalty for a wrong guess
  • About 30% of questions are tied to case studies presenting a real-world AI development or deployment scenario — these take longer to read than a straight definitional question
  • Total time: 3 hours, including one optional 15-minute break midway through — roughly 165 minutes of actual testing time for 100 questions
  • Delivered via Pearson VUE, either at a testing center or through OnVUE remote proctoring
  • Scoring: reported on a scaled 100–500 range; 300 is the passing cut score; you receive a section-by-section performance breakdown after completion

  • Budget extra time for case-study questions — if the testing platform allows flagging/review, mark a tough one and come back rather than stalling your pace
  • Since you can't tell scored from unscored questions, don't try to guess which is which — treat every question the same

Domain I — Foundations of AI Governance

16–20 questions (~20% of exam)

What AI governance is, and the principles and building blocks used to construct a governance program — applicable regardless of industry, sector or organization size.

I.A — What AI Is, and Why It Needs Governance

Defining AI

  • AI is generally defined as a machine-based system that, for a given set of objectives, generates outputs such as predictions, recommendations, content or decisions influencing real or virtual environments
  • Key subtypes to distinguish: rule-based/expert systems vs. machine learning; narrow AI vs. general AI; predictive/classic ML vs. generative AI; single-modal vs. multimodal; and increasingly, agentic AI — systems that can plan and take multi-step actions toward a goal with reduced human intervention

Risks and Harms of AI

  • Misalignment: the system optimizes for a proxy objective that diverges from the actual intended goal
  • Ethics and bias risk: outputs that discriminate or entrench unfairness against individuals or groups
  • Complexity and scalability risk: a flaw that would be minor in one instance becomes systemic once a model is deployed at scale across millions of decisions
  • Harms can land on individuals (denied opportunity), groups (disparate impact), organizations (legal/reputational liability) and society (erosion of trust, labor displacement, information integrity)

What Makes AI Uniquely Hard to Govern

  • Complexity: many interacting components (data, model, infrastructure, human processes) that are each independently hard to fully specify
  • Opacity: many models (especially deep learning) are effectively "black boxes" whose internal reasoning isn't directly inspectable
  • Autonomy: some systems act with limited human review, especially agentic systems that chain decisions together
  • Speed and scale: AI can make or influence far more decisions, far faster, than manual human processes — errors propagate before they're caught
  • Potential for harm or misuse: dual-use risk — capabilities intended for benign purposes can be repurposed maliciously
  • Data dependency: system behavior is a function of training data, so data quality/representativeness problems become model problems
  • Probabilistic vs. deterministic outputs: unlike traditional software, the same AI input can produce different outputs, complicating testing, validation and reproducibility

Common Principles of Responsible AI

  • Fairness: avoiding unjust discrimination in outcomes and treatment
  • Safety and reliability: the system performs consistently as intended, and fails safely when it doesn't
  • Privacy and security: protecting personal data and defending against adversarial manipulation
  • Transparency and explainability: stakeholders can understand that AI is in use and how/why it reached a given output
  • Accountability: a clear, traceable chain of responsibility for AI decisions and outcomes
  • Human-centricity: AI serves human well-being and preserves meaningful human agency over consequential decisions

I.B — Organizational Roles and Expectations

Roles and Responsibilities

  • Governance requires clearly assigned ownership at every stage — ambiguity about "whose job this is" is one of the most common root causes of AI governance failure
  • Typical roles: executive sponsor/Chief AI Officer, AI governance/ethics committee, model owners, data stewards, legal/privacy/compliance, security, and the business unit deploying the system

Cross-Functional Collaboration

  • Effective governance pulls in legal, data science, security, product, and the affected business function — no single discipline has full visibility into all AI risks
  • Diversity of expertise and perspective on a governance body reduces blind spots (e.g., a legal-only review may miss a technical robustness gap, and vice versa)

Training and Awareness

  • All stakeholders — not just technical staff — need role-appropriate training on AI terminology, organizational strategy, and their governance obligations
  • A trained workforce is both a risk control (fewer inadvertent misuse incidents) and often a legal expectation under emerging AI laws

Right-Sizing Governance

  • Governance approach should scale with company size, AI maturity, industry, the nature of products/services, business objectives, and risk tolerance — a five-person startup and a regulated bank should not run identical governance processes

Developers, Providers, Deployers and Users

  • Developer/Provider: builds and/or places the AI system on the market — carries obligations around design, testing, documentation, and conformity
  • Deployer: puts a system into use under its own authority — carries obligations around appropriate use, monitoring, and oversight
  • User: the end user interacting with the system's outputs
  • Many organizations occupy more than one role simultaneously (e.g., fine-tuning a third-party foundation model makes you both a deployer of the base model and, in some frameworks, a provider of the resulting system) — governance obligations shift accordingly

I.C — Policies and Procedures Across the AI Lifecycle

Lifecycle-Wide Policy Coverage

  • Governance policy should span every lifecycle stage: use case assessment, risk management, ethics by design, data acquisition and use, model/system development, training and testing, deployment and monitoring, documentation and reporting, and incident management
  • "Ethics by design" means embedding ethical review at the point of design, not bolting it on after a model is built

Updating Existing Policies for AI

  • AI doesn't only need new policy — it stresses existing policy. Data privacy, security, data governance and intellectual property policies typically need to be reevaluated and updated to explicitly address AI-specific scenarios (e.g., can training data be reused for a new model purpose under the original privacy notice?)

Third-Party and Vendor Risk

  • Procurement, supply chain, HR, and acceptable-use policies need AI-specific updates and assessments — an organization can incur significant liability from a vendor's AI system even without building any AI itself
  • Contracts should address audit rights, model change notification, liability allocation, and data handling

Domain II — Laws, Standards and Frameworks

19–23 questions (~25% of exam)

How existing (non-AI-specific) laws apply to AI, the main elements of AI-specific laws, and the leading industry standards and frameworks.

II.A — Existing Data Privacy Laws Applied to AI

  • Transparency, choice, lawful basis, purpose limitation: AI systems must still be built on a lawful basis for processing personal data, and data collected for one purpose generally can't be silently repurposed to train an unrelated model
  • Data minimization and privacy by design: collect only the data needed for the stated AI purpose, and build privacy safeguards into the system from the start rather than retrofitting them
  • Data controller obligations: privacy impact assessments, due diligence on third-party processors, cross-border data transfer mechanisms, honoring data subject rights (access, deletion, correction), rules on automated decision-making, incident management, breach notification, and recordkeeping all apply when personal data feeds an AI system
  • Sensitive/special category data: biometric data (e.g., face or voice used for identification) typically triggers heightened consent and processing requirements

II.B — Other Existing Laws Applied to AI

  • Intellectual property law: governs whether copyrighted or proprietary data can lawfully be used to train an AI model, and raises open questions about ownership of AI-generated output
  • Nondiscrimination law: long-standing protections in employment, credit, lending, housing, and insurance apply fully to AI-driven decisions in those areas — an algorithm producing a disparate outcome is not a defense against a discrimination claim
  • Consumer protection law: prohibitions on unfair and deceptive acts or practices apply to AI-powered marketing, chatbots, and pricing — e.g., a chatbot falsely claiming to be human, or a "dark pattern" AI upsell
  • Product liability law: design and manufacturing defect theories can apply to AI systems embedded in products, raising questions about who is liable when a flawed model causes physical or financial harm

II.C — Main Elements of AI-Specific Laws

Risk Classification

  • Most AI-specific laws (most prominently the EU AI Act) use a tiered risk classification: prohibited, high, limited, and minimal risk — with obligations scaling to the tier
  • Know representative examples of what falls in each tier (e.g., social scoring = prohibited; hiring/credit systems = high risk; chatbots = limited risk with a disclosure duty; spam filters = minimal risk)
  • AI law is a global patchwork, not just the EU — e.g., South Korea's AI Basic Law and various U.S. federal/state laws also regulate AI in the private sector, generally following similar risk-based logic

Core Compliance Requirements

  • Risk management: an ongoing, documented process to identify and mitigate risk across the lifecycle, not a one-time checklist
  • Data governance: requirements on training/testing/validation data quality, relevance, and representativeness
  • Technical documentation: records sufficient for a regulator or auditor to understand how the system works and was validated
  • Conformity/impact assessments: formal evaluations (sometimes third-party) confirming a system meets applicable requirements before or during deployment
  • Recordkeeping: retained logs and evidence supporting ongoing compliance
  • Human oversight: a meaningful (not token) ability for a person to understand, intervene in, or override the system
  • Transparency and notification: disclosing that AI is in use and providing understandable information about it to affected people
  • Quality management: an internal system ensuring consistent compliance over time, not just at launch

General-Purpose AI (GPAI)

  • Foundation/general-purpose models (e.g., large language models) usually carry distinct obligations from narrow, task-specific "high-risk" systems, often including extra scrutiny for models presenting systemic risk (frequently tied to compute or capability thresholds)

Enforcement and Organizational Context

  • Know the enforcement mechanisms and penalty structures (fines, market withdrawal, bans) that back these laws
  • Obligations differ by role in the value chain: providers (build/place on market), deployers (put into use), importers, and distributors each have distinct duties

II.D — Industry Standards and Frameworks

OECD AI Principles

  • Influential international framework (adopted by 40+ countries) establishing principles for trustworthy AI: inclusive growth, human-centered values and fairness, transparency, robustness/safety/security, and accountability — it underpins much of the G20 AI policy language and many national AI strategies

NIST AI Risk Management Framework and Playbook

  • Four core functions: Govern (build a risk-aware culture and structure), Map (identify context and risks of a specific system), Measure (analyze and track risk with qualitative/quantitative methods), Manage (prioritize and act on risk)
  • Each function breaks down into more granular categories and subcategories — concrete, actionable practices beneath the four high-level functions (e.g., under Map: "context is established and understood"; under Measure: "appropriate methods are identified to measure trustworthiness")
  • The companion NIST AI RMF Playbook offers practical suggestions for implementing each subcategory

Core ISO/IEC AI Standards

  • ISO/IEC 22989: establishes foundational AI concepts and terminology — the common vocabulary used across the other AI standards
  • ISO/IEC 42001: the certifiable AI Management System (AIMS) standard — the AI analog to ISO 27001 for information security — specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, and continually improving an organizational AI management system
  • ISO/IEC 42005: guidance specifically on AI system impact assessment — how to evaluate the potential consequences of an AI system on individuals, groups, and society

Domain III — Governing AI Development

21–25 questions (~27% of exam)

The AI governance professional's responsibilities across designing, building, training, testing and maintaining AI systems.

III.A — Governing the Design and Build of the AI System

  • Define the business context and use case before any modeling work starts — an AI project without a clearly defined problem is ungovernable by default
  • Perform or review an impact assessment on the proposed system, covering who could be affected and how
  • Apply policy and ethics to design/build decisions: purpose of the AI, requirements gathering, architecture and model selection, human oversight design, data analysis, metric and threshold evaluation (what "good performance" means and where the line is), stakeholder engagement/feedback, and operational controls
  • Identify and manage risk using structured tools: a probability/severity harms matrix (plot likelihood against severity to prioritize mitigation), a risk mitigation hierarchy (eliminate the risk first, then reduce, then control, then merely warn/accept as a last resort), stakeholder mapping, use-case evaluation, benchmarking against alternatives, and pre-deployment pilots/testing
  • Document the design and build process — this evidence is what later establishes compliance and supports incident investigations

III.B — Governing Data, Training and Testing

  • Data governance requirements: assess and document the organization's lawful right to collect and use the data (license, consent, or other legal basis), and evaluate data quality, quantity, integrity, and fit-for-purpose for the specific model task
  • Data lineage and provenance: establish and document where data came from and how it has been transformed — essential for both compliance and root-cause debugging later
  • Training and testing plan: should cover unit testing, integration testing, validation, performance testing, security testing, bias testing, and interpretability testing — treating only "accuracy" as the test criterion is a common and serious gap
  • Identify and manage issues/risks that surface during training and testing (e.g., discovering a subgroup performance gap before launch, not after)
  • Document the training and testing process to validate results, establish compliance, and support risk management

III.C — Governing Release, Monitoring and Maintenance

  • Readiness assessment for release: confirm the system is ready for production, including creating the model card (documentation of intended use, performance, and limitations) and satisfying any applicable conformity requirements
  • Continuous monitoring: track the system in production on an ongoing basis, with a regular cadence for maintenance, updates, and retraining — governance doesn't end at launch
  • Periodic assessment activities: audits, red teaming (adversarial testing by a team simulating attackers/misuse), threat modeling (systematically mapping how the system could be attacked or misused), and security testing
  • Incident, issue and risk management: a defined process to detect, document, and respond to problems after release
  • Root-cause collaboration: work cross-functionally to understand why incidents occur — common causes include brittleness, lack of robustness, poor-quality data, insufficient testing, and model or data drift
  • Public disclosures: meet transparency obligations through technical documentation, instructions for use provided to deployers, and post-market monitoring plans

Domain IV — Governing AI Deployment and Use

21–25 questions (~27% of exam)

Responsibilities around selecting, deploying and using an AI system responsibly — whether it's a proprietary model your organization built or a third-party model you're putting into use.

IV.A — Evaluating Deployment Factors and Risks

  • Use case context: business objectives, performance requirements, data availability, ethical considerations, and workforce readiness all shape whether and how to deploy
  • Model type differences matter for governance: classic/predictive vs. generative, proprietary vs. open source, small vs. large, and single-language/unimodal vs. multimodal models each carry distinct risk profiles and oversight needs
  • Deployment options: cloud vs. on-premise vs. edge deployment change the data-control and security calculus; and a model can be used as-is or adapted via fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), agentic architectures, or other techniques — each adaptation technique introduces its own governance considerations (e.g., RAG pulls in external content at inference time that itself needs governance)

IV.B — Assessing the AI System Before Deployment

  • Impact assessment: perform or review one on the specific system being considered for deployment, not just the underlying model in the abstract
  • Vendor and licensing review: identify and evaluate key contractual terms and risks — data use rights, liability allocation, service levels, audit rights, and change-notification obligations
  • Proprietary model deployment: deploying your own model (rather than a third party's) generally increases both obligations and potential liability, since you have more control and therefore more responsibility for outcomes

IV.C — Governing Deployment and Ongoing Use

  • Apply policy at deployment: data governance, risk management, issue management, and user training all need to be in place before go-live, not added afterward
  • Continuous monitoring and maintenance: establish a regular schedule for monitoring, updates, and retraining throughout the system's operational life
  • Periodic assessment: audits, red teaming, threat modeling, and security testing should continue after deployment, not stop at launch
  • Documentation: incidents, issues, risks, and post-market monitoring plans all need to be recorded and kept current
  • Forecast secondary/unintended uses: anticipate and reduce the risk of the system being used for something other than its intended purpose, and the downstream harms that could follow
  • External communication plans: have a plan for communicating with affected users, regulators, or the public if something goes wrong
  • Deactivation/localization controls: maintain the policy and technical ability to shut down or geographically restrict an AI system when required — e.g., due to a new regulatory requirement or a performance/safety issue

Exam Tips & Quick-Reference Glossary

How the Exam Is Weighted

  • Domains III and IV (governing development and governing deployment/use) together make up roughly half the exam — hands-on lifecycle governance is tested more heavily than pure definitions
  • Per IAPP's own guidance, most questions sit at the Bloom's Taxonomy "remember/understand" and "apply/analyze" levels — expect scenario-style questions ("a team wants to deploy X, what should they do first?"), not just term-matching

Acronym Cheat Sheet

  • AIGP — AI Governance Professional (the certification)
  • AIMS — AI Management System (ISO/IEC 42001)
  • RMF — Risk Management Framework (NIST)
  • GPAI — General-Purpose AI
  • RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation
  • DPIA — Data Protection Impact Assessment
  • XAI — Explainable AI

Commonly Confused Pairs

  • Provider vs. deployer: provider builds/places on the market; deployer puts into use under its own authority — many orgs are both at once
  • Risk mitigation hierarchy vs. harms matrix: the harms matrix helps you prioritize risks (probability × severity); the mitigation hierarchy tells you what order of response to prefer (eliminate → reduce → control → warn/accept)
  • Red teaming vs. threat modeling: threat modeling is the systematic, often desk-based mapping of how a system could be attacked; red teaming is the active, hands-on attempt to actually attack/misuse it
  • Model card vs. impact assessment: a model card documents what the system is and how it performs; an impact assessment evaluates who could be harmed and how, before or during deployment

Study Strategy

  • For each performance indicator, practice explaining why it matters (the harm it prevents), not just its name
  • Walk through the AI lifecycle end-to-end (design → data/training → release/monitor → deployment/use) and be able to name the governance activity expected at each stage
  • Regulatory content moves fast — treat specific law names/thresholds as illustrative examples of a risk-tiered pattern, and verify current detail against the official BoK/exam content outline before test day
