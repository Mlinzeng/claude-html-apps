1. AI Governance Fundamentals

What is AI Governance?

AI Governance is the set of processes, policies, and systems used to ensure that AI systems are developed, deployed, and operated responsibly and ethically.

  • Encompasses risk management, compliance, and ethical frameworks
  • Involves multiple stakeholders: businesses, regulators, technologists, and society
  • Addresses transparency, accountability, and fairness
  • Balances innovation with risk mitigation

Governance vs. Ethics vs. Risk Management

  • AI Ethics: The normative question of what an AI system should do — values like fairness, dignity, and non-maleficence
  • AI Risk Management: The operational discipline of identifying, measuring, and mitigating harms across the AI lifecycle
  • AI Governance: The overarching structure — policies, roles, controls, and accountability mechanisms — that operationalizes ethics and risk management into enforceable practice

Why Governance Matters

  • Trust: Users, customers, and regulators need assurance that AI decisions are sound and contestable
  • Legal exposure: Ungoverned AI creates liability under anti-discrimination, consumer protection, and data protection law
  • Operational resilience: Governance catches model failures (drift, bias, security gaps) before they cause harm at scale
  • Competitive advantage: Mature governance enables faster, safer scaling of AI rather than slowing it down

The AI Lifecycle (governance touches every stage)

  • Problem definition & scoping: Determine if AI is appropriate; define intended use and out-of-scope uses
  • Data collection & preparation: Sourcing, labeling, consent, representativeness
  • Model development & training: Algorithm selection, hyperparameter tuning, validation
  • Testing & validation: Performance, fairness, robustness, and security testing before release
  • Deployment: Release gating, human-in-the-loop design, rollback plans
  • Monitoring & maintenance: Drift detection, incident response, periodic re-validation
  • Decommissioning: Retiring models safely, preserving audit records

Key Terminology

  • Model: The trained artifact that maps inputs to outputs
  • Training data: Data used to fit model parameters; distinct from validation/test data
  • Inference: The act of running a trained model on new input to produce a prediction
  • Ground truth: The verified, correct label or outcome used to evaluate model accuracy
  • Foundation model / general-purpose AI: A large model trained on broad data and adaptable to many downstream tasks (e.g., LLMs) — subject to distinct regulatory treatment (e.g., under the EU AI Act)
  • Deployer vs. Provider: The provider builds/places an AI system on the market; the deployer uses it under its own authority — governance obligations differ between the two

2. Regulatory Landscape

Key Regulations and Standards

  • EU AI Act: Classifies AI by risk levels; regulates high-risk applications
  • GDPR: Addresses data privacy and algorithmic transparency
  • Algorithm Auditing: Requirements for testing and validation
  • National Standards: Emerging frameworks in various countries
  • Industry Standards: ISO/IEC standards for AI quality and safety

EU AI Act — Risk Tier Detail

  • Unacceptable risk (banned): Social scoring by governments, manipulative subliminal techniques, real-time biometric identification in public spaces (with narrow exceptions), untargeted scraping of facial images
  • High risk: AI in employment/hiring, credit scoring, education admissions, law enforcement, critical infrastructure, medical devices — requires conformity assessment, risk management system, technical documentation, human oversight, and registration in an EU database
  • Limited risk: Chatbots, deepfakes, emotion recognition — subject to transparency obligations (must disclose AI is being used)
  • Minimal risk: Spam filters, AI-enabled video games — largely unregulated
  • General-Purpose AI (GPAI): Separate obligations for foundation model providers, with extra requirements for models presenting "systemic risk" (based on compute thresholds)

GDPR & AI

  • Article 22: Individuals have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing (including profiling) that has legal or similarly significant effects
  • DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment): Required for high-risk processing, including many AI use cases
  • Data minimization & purpose limitation: Constrain how training data can be collected and reused
  • Right to explanation: Debated concept; GDPR grants a right to "meaningful information about the logic involved" in automated decisions

NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)

  • Govern: Cultivate a risk-aware culture and organizational structure
  • Map: Identify context, intended use, and risks of a specific AI system
  • Measure: Analyze, assess, and track risks using quantitative/qualitative methods
  • Manage: Prioritize and act on risks, allocate resources, plan responses

ISO/IEC Standards

  • ISO/IEC 42001: First international AI Management System (AIMS) standard — certifiable, analogous to ISO 27001 for security
  • ISO/IEC 23894: AI risk management guidance
  • ISO/IEC 23053: Framework for AI systems using machine learning
  • ISO/IEC TR 24028: Overview of trustworthiness in AI

Other Notable Frameworks & Laws

  • OECD AI Principles: International standard for trustworthy AI, adopted by 40+ countries
  • US Colorado AI Act: First comprehensive US state law regulating "high-risk" AI systems and algorithmic discrimination
  • NYC Local Law 144: Requires bias audits for automated employment decision tools
  • China's Generative AI Measures & Algorithm Recommendation regulations: Require security assessments and algorithm registration
  • Canada's AIDA (Artificial Intelligence and Data Act): Proposed risk-based framework for "high-impact" systems
  • Sector-specific rules: FDA oversight of AI/ML-based medical devices (SaMD), financial services model risk management guidance (e.g., SR 11-7 in the US)

3. Risk Assessment & Management

Identifying and Mitigating AI Risks

  • Bias and Fairness: Detecting and preventing discriminatory outcomes
  • Transparency: Explainability (XAI) of AI decision-making
  • Data Security: Protecting training and operational data
  • Model Robustness: Testing against adversarial inputs
  • Accountability: Clear responsibility chains for AI decisions

Types of Bias in AI Systems

  • Historical bias: Training data reflects past societal inequities (e.g., biased lending history)
  • Representation bias: Training data underrepresents certain groups
  • Measurement bias: Proxy variables poorly capture the concept intended (e.g., using arrest records as a proxy for crime)
  • Aggregation bias: A single model applied uniformly fails to capture differences across subgroups
  • Evaluation bias: Benchmark/test data doesn't reflect the deployment population

Fairness Metrics

  • Demographic parity: Positive outcome rates are equal across groups
  • Equalized odds: True positive and false positive rates are equal across groups
  • Predictive parity: Precision is equal across groups
  • Individual fairness: Similar individuals receive similar outcomes
  • Note: these metrics can mathematically conflict — satisfying one often means violating another, so tradeoffs must be documented and justified

Algorithmic Impact Assessments (AIA)

  • Structured, documented evaluation of an AI system's potential impact on individuals and society before deployment
  • Typically covers: purpose, affected populations, data sources, risk of harm, mitigation measures, and monitoring plan
  • Increasingly required by law (e.g., Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making, various US state laws)

Security Risks Specific to AI

  • Data poisoning: Adversary corrupts training data to manipulate model behavior
  • Adversarial examples: Inputs subtly perturbed to cause misclassification
  • Model inversion: Attacker reconstructs sensitive training data from model outputs
  • Membership inference: Attacker determines whether a specific record was in the training set
  • Model/IP theft: Extracting a proprietary model via repeated querying
  • Prompt injection (for LLMs): Malicious input manipulates a model into ignoring instructions or leaking data

Risk Assessment Methodology

  • Likelihood × Severity: Classic risk-scoring approach adapted for AI harms (individual, group, and societal levels)
  • Risk tiering: Categorize systems (e.g., minimal/limited/high/unacceptable, mirroring EU AI Act) to calibrate the level of scrutiny and controls required
  • Continuous reassessment: Risk isn't static — model updates, new data, or new use contexts require re-assessment

4. Ethical AI Principles

Core Ethical Frameworks

  • Fairness: Ensuring equal treatment across demographic groups
  • Transparency: Clear communication about AI capabilities and limitations
  • Accountability: Clear ownership and responsibility for AI outcomes
  • Privacy: Protecting individual data and avoiding misuse
  • Human Agency: Maintaining human control over critical decisions
  • Sustainability: Considering environmental and societal impact

Influential Ethics Frameworks

  • OECD AI Principles (2019): Inclusive growth, human-centered values, transparency, robustness/safety, accountability — widely referenced baseline for national policy
  • IEEE Ethically Aligned Design: Engineering-oriented framework emphasizing human well-being as a success metric, not just performance
  • Asilomar AI Principles: Research-community principles covering safety, failure transparency, value alignment, and long-term risk
  • UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI: First global standard-setting instrument on AI ethics, adopted by 190+ member states

Explainability vs. Interpretability

  • Interpretability: The model's internal logic is inherently understandable (e.g., a decision tree or linear regression)
  • Explainability: A separate technique explains a black-box model's output after the fact (e.g., SHAP, LIME)
  • Global vs. local explanations: Global explains overall model behavior; local explains a single prediction

Tensions and Tradeoffs Between Principles

  • Accuracy vs. fairness: Debiasing techniques can reduce raw predictive accuracy for the majority group
  • Transparency vs. IP/security: Full model disclosure can expose trade secrets or enable adversarial attacks
  • Privacy vs. fairness auditing: Measuring bias often requires collecting the very demographic data privacy principles discourage collecting
  • Personalization vs. autonomy: Highly optimized recommendation systems can undermine human agency through manipulation

Human-Centered Design Concepts

  • Human-in-the-loop (HITL): A person reviews/approves each AI decision before it takes effect
  • Human-on-the-loop: A person monitors and can intervene/override, but doesn't approve every decision
  • Human-out-of-the-loop: Fully automated, no real-time human oversight — reserved for low-risk, well-validated use cases

5. Organizational AI Governance

Implementing Governance Structures

  • AI Governance Committees: Cross-functional oversight bodies
  • Roles & Responsibilities: Chief AI Officer, AI Ethics Board, Model Owners
  • Policies & Procedures: Standards for development, deployment, and monitoring
  • Training & Awareness: Educating staff on responsible AI practices
  • Documentation: Model cards, risk assessments, audit trails

The Three Lines of Defense Model (adapted for AI)

  • First line: Data scientists and product teams who build and operate models — own day-to-day risk controls
  • Second line: Risk, compliance, and AI governance functions that set policy and independently review/challenge first-line work
  • Third line: Internal audit — provides independent assurance that governance controls are actually working

Key Roles in Detail

  • Chief AI Officer (CAIO): Executive accountable for AI strategy, risk, and value across the organization
  • AI Ethics Board: Reviews high-risk or novel use cases, adjudicates escalations, sets ethical policy
  • Model Owner: Accountable for a specific model's performance, risk, and lifecycle over time
  • Model Validator / Independent Reviewer: Tests and challenges models independently of the build team (separation of duties)
  • Data Steward: Owns data quality, lineage, and appropriate use for a given dataset
  • AI Risk Officer: Embeds AI-specific risk assessment into the enterprise risk management function

AI Governance Maturity

  • Ad hoc: No formal policy; governance depends on individual judgment
  • Developing: Policies exist but are inconsistently applied; limited inventory of AI systems
  • Defined: Standardized processes, a central AI inventory/registry, and defined roles across the lifecycle
  • Managed: Metrics-driven oversight; risk tiering applied consistently; regular audits
  • Optimized: Continuous improvement; governance scales with AI adoption without becoming a bottleneck

The AI System Inventory / Registry

  • Centralized, living catalog of every AI system in use, its risk tier, owner, purpose, and status
  • Foundational control — you cannot govern what you cannot see; increasingly a regulatory requirement (e.g., EU AI Act database for high-risk systems)

Vendor & Third-Party AI Governance

  • Procurement due diligence: request model cards, bias audit results, and security attestations from vendors
  • Contractual controls: right-to-audit clauses, liability allocation, notification obligations for model changes
  • Deployer obligations often apply even when an organization didn't build the underlying model (e.g., fine-tuned or embedded third-party AI)

6. Model Monitoring & Governance

Lifecycle Management

  • Continuous Monitoring: Tracking model performance and drift over time
  • Audit Trails: Recording all decisions and model changes
  • Model Governance Boards: Review and approval processes
  • Incident Response: Protocols for handling model failures or misuse
  • Versioning & Updates: Managing model evolution and rollback strategies

Types of Drift

  • Data drift (covariate shift): The statistical distribution of input features changes over time
  • Concept drift: The relationship between inputs and the target outcome changes (the thing being predicted itself evolves)
  • Label drift: The distribution of the target variable itself shifts
  • Upstream/pipeline drift: Changes in a feature's source system silently alter its meaning or scale

Monitoring Metrics

  • Performance metrics: Accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC — tracked over time, not just at launch
  • Fairness metrics: Ongoing subgroup performance comparisons, not a one-time check
  • Operational metrics: Latency, throughput, uptime, error rates
  • Population Stability Index (PSI) / KL divergence: Statistical measures commonly used to quantify drift
  • Business/outcome metrics: Whether the model is still delivering the intended real-world value

MLOps & Governance Integration

  • CI/CD for models: Automated testing gates (performance, fairness, security) before promotion to production
  • Champion/challenger testing: New model versions run alongside the production model before full rollout
  • Canary/staged rollout: Gradually expose a new model to a subset of traffic to limit blast radius
  • Rollback plans: Predefined criteria and mechanism to revert to a prior model version

Incident Response for AI

  • Detection: Automated alerts for drift, fairness violations, or anomalous outputs
  • Triage & severity classification: Assess scope of harm (individual vs. systemic)
  • Containment: Disable/roll back the model, add human review, or restrict scope
  • Root cause analysis & remediation: Fix underlying data, model, or process issue
  • Disclosure: Notify affected individuals/regulators where legally required
  • Post-incident review: Update governance controls to prevent recurrence

Model Decommissioning

  • Formal retirement process — an unmonitored legacy model left running is a common source of ungoverned risk ("model sprawl")
  • Preserve documentation and audit trails post-retirement for legal/regulatory retention requirements

7. AI Governance Tools & Technologies

Practical Implementation Tools

  • Explainability Tools: SHAP, LIME, Integrated Gradients
  • Fairness Testing: Bias detection and mitigation frameworks
  • Model Governance Platforms: MLflow, Kubeflow, DataRobot
  • Data Management: Data catalogs, lineage tracking, quality monitoring
  • Compliance Tools: Audit logging, policy enforcement automation

Explainability Techniques — How They Work

  • SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations): Game-theory-based method that attributes a prediction's outcome to each input feature's contribution, both locally and globally
  • LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations): Approximates a complex model locally with a simple, interpretable one around a specific prediction
  • Integrated Gradients: Attributes a neural network's output to input features by integrating gradients along a path from a baseline input
  • Counterfactual explanations: Show the minimal change to an input that would flip the model's decision (e.g., "if income were $2,000 higher, the loan would be approved")

Fairness & Bias Detection Toolkits

  • IBM AI Fairness 360 (AIF360): Open-source library of fairness metrics and bias mitigation algorithms
  • Google What-If Tool / Fairness Indicators: Visual, no-code exploration of model behavior across slices of data
  • Microsoft Fairlearn: Python package for assessing and improving fairness in ML models
  • Aequitas: Bias audit toolkit focused on policy and public-sector use cases

MLOps & Model Governance Platforms

  • MLflow: Open-source experiment tracking, model registry, and packaging
  • Kubeflow: Kubernetes-native platform for orchestrating ML pipelines
  • DataRobot / Databricks / SageMaker Model Monitor: Enterprise platforms with built-in monitoring, governance, and approval workflows
  • Model registries: Central source of truth for model versions, lineage, and approval status

Data Governance Tools

  • Data catalogs (e.g., Collibra, Alation): Inventory of data assets with metadata and ownership
  • Lineage tracking: Traces data from source through transformations to model input, critical for root-cause analysis and audits
  • Data quality monitoring: Automated checks for completeness, consistency, and drift in input data

Compliance & Documentation Artifacts

  • Model cards: Standardized documentation of a model's intended use, performance, limitations, and ethical considerations (popularized by Google's Model Card framework)
  • Datasheets for datasets: Documentation of dataset provenance, composition, and collection process
  • Audit logging: Immutable records of model decisions, inputs, and version used — supports investigations and regulatory audits
  • Policy-as-code: Automated enforcement of governance rules (e.g., blocking deployment of a model that fails a bias threshold) directly in the CI/CD pipeline

8. Exam Tips & Quick-Reference Glossary

High-Yield Numbers & Facts

  • NIST AI RMF has 4 core functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage
  • EU AI Act defines 4 risk tiers: Unacceptable, High, Limited, Minimal (plus a separate track for General-Purpose AI)
  • ISO/IEC 42001 = certifiable AI Management System standard (like 27001, but for AI)
  • ISO/IEC 23894 = AI risk management guidance
  • GDPR Article 22 = right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal/significant effects
  • OECD AI Principles were adopted by 40+ countries and underpin the G20 AI principles
  • NYC Local Law 144 = mandatory bias audits for automated employment decision tools

Acronym Cheat Sheet

  • XAI — Explainable AI
  • SHAP — SHapley Additive exPlanations
  • LIME — Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations
  • AIMS — AI Management System (ISO/IEC 42001)
  • AIA — Algorithmic Impact Assessment
  • DPIA — Data Protection Impact Assessment
  • RMF — Risk Management Framework
  • GPAI — General-Purpose AI
  • HITL / HOTL — Human-in-the-Loop / Human-on-the-Loop
  • MLOps — Machine Learning Operations
  • PSI — Population Stability Index (drift metric)
  • SaMD — Software as a Medical Device
  • AIDA — (Canada) Artificial Intelligence and Data Act

Commonly Confused Pairs

  • Bias vs. Fairness: Bias is a measurable statistical property (a skew); fairness is the normative judgment about whether that skew is acceptable
  • Interpretability vs. Explainability: Interpretability is built-in transparency; explainability is a post-hoc technique applied to a black box
  • Data drift vs. Concept drift: Data drift = inputs change; concept drift = the input-output relationship itself changes
  • Provider vs. Deployer (EU AI Act): Provider builds/places the system on the market; deployer uses it under their own authority — obligations differ
  • Governance vs. Compliance: Compliance is meeting a specific external rule; governance is the broader internal system that produces compliance (and more) consistently

Study Strategy

  • Focus on why a control exists (the harm it prevents), not just its name — exam questions often test application, not recall
  • Map each regulation to its risk-based structure — most modern AI laws (EU AI Act, Colorado AI Act, Canada's AIDA) share a tiered, risk-based logic
  • Practice distinguishing overlapping roles (Model Owner vs. Model Validator vs. Data Steward) — these are frequently tested
  • Review the fairness-metric tradeoffs carefully — questions often present a scenario where two fairness definitions conflict
